# Decision rules
These rules are a maintainer synthesis of the inspected sources in [source register](source-register.md). Source keys identify support, not claims of universal agreement. Cite the relevant rule and source passage when applying it, together with the case inputs.
## R1 Establish the use and intended claim
Separate the research question from the questionnaire item. Ask what decision, learning objective, or monitoring use the evidence serves. For a decision study, identify the result that could change action. Permit a legitimate discovery objective without inventing a numeric threshold. Consider whether existing evidence already answers the question. [S1; S8. Learning-objective exception: package synthesis.]
Connect each research goal to the required evidence, proposed item or measure, and anticipated analysis. Check for unanswered goals and items with no stated purpose. At this gate, record the mapping provisionally; detailed item design belongs in the measurement and questionnaire handoff. [S11, ch.1, reader pp.34–37; S12, ch.1, printed pp.12–14.]
Identify the target population, unit, period, and priority subgroups. Keep people, accounts, organizations, sessions, and repeated observations distinct. Preserve this definition when discussing compromises. [S2, operationalization and external validity; application policy.]
## R2 Check answerability and measurement
Define the construct, relevant experience, respondent, and period before selecting an item. Record existing measures considered, their response options, and proposed adaptations. An adaptation needs review; familiarity does not establish suitability for a new use. [S12, ch.6, printed pp.64–66; S2, construct validity.]
Check four response stages: understanding, finding or recalling an answer, judging what answer to give, and expressing the answer in the available format. Record plausible problems in the central measures without inventing failures. Consider cognitive testing or discovery before standardizing uncertain categories. [S11, ch.3, reader pp.100–101; S12, ch.9, printed pp.91–92; S3; S4.]
Separate testing interpretation from testing the collection process. Cognitive testing examines how intended respondents understand and answer. An operational pilot checks invitations, access, routing, timing, data capture, and whether the planned analysis can be produced. Record proportionate plans, unresolved issues, and conditions for proceeding; neither test substitutes for the other. Do not impose a fixed pilot percentage. [S11, ch.3, reader pp.119–120 and ch.5, pp.212–213; S12, ch.18, printed pp.296–298; proportionality: package policy.]
Distinguish perceived ease, reported behavior, observed performance, and future outcomes. An answer about perceived understanding is evidence about that perception; demonstrated understanding requires an appropriate performance measure. Check the intended use of a familiar or adapted scale instead of assuming validity transfers. [S2, construct validity; S8, proxies and leading indicators.]
## R3 Match the design to the claim
- Defined attitudes or reported experiences: consider a survey after measurement and sampling checks. [S3; S8.]
- Actual use or task performance: assess valid behavioral records or observation first. When records are incomplete, consider bounded self-report or mixed methods and disclose the limitation. [S8, suitability; S11, ch.1, reader pp.39–40; S12, ch.1, printed pp.11–12; S2, operationalization.]
- Unknown categories or contextual problems: consider exploratory work before estimating prevalence. Do not route solely on the words “what” or “why.” [S4.]
- Product-change effects: require a justified causal design. A survey can measure an experimental outcome; asking respondents for causes does not isolate a mechanism. Check differential response even with randomized treatment. [S2, internal validity; S4; S8, causal surveys.]
- Future adoption or retention: separate stated intention from verified prediction. Request validation against the eventual outcome, fresh-data testing, and relevant calibration checks. [S8, proxies and leading indicators.]
For mixed methods, specify the sequence or concurrent arrangement, the question each component answers, and the integration point. Explain how earlier findings change later measurement, or how combined findings change interpretation. Simply listing interviews and a survey leaves this dependency unresolved. [S13, “Ways to Mix Methods,” reader pp.37–38; “Research Design,” pp.50–53; gate implementation: synthesis.]
These are conditional routes. Explain what the selected method can establish and what remains unanswered.
## R4 Check coverage and selection
Distinguish population, reachable frame, invitees, respondents, and analytic sample. Assess coverage gaps, sampling variability, nonresponse differences, and measurement problems separately. A sufficient sample-size calculation leaves the other risks unresolved. Large samples, opt-in panels, quotas, or high response alone do not establish population coverage or absence of nonresponse error. [S12, ch.2, printed pp.20–24; S5; S2, non-response bias.]
Record invitation channel separately from completion mode, such as email invitations to a web questionnaire. Check who the combination reaches or excludes and whether respondents can complete the answering task. Route implications for burden, accessibility, identity, and operational testing to the relevant specialist. [S11, Spotlight G, reader pp.139–142; handoff: package synthesis.]
For weighting, ask which benchmarks and assumptions support the adjustment. Check sparse or absent cells and the implications for precision. Do not infer that demographic adjustment recovers an absent population. Route detailed weighting decisions to sampling expertise. [S10, population weights and variable selection; S5. Absent-population restriction: conservative application.]
A small, bounded population may support useful descriptive work. A census invitation is only a recruitment attempt; record achieved coverage and nonresponse. Do not impose a universal minimum sample. [S2, external validity and non-response bias; package application.]
## R5 Check achievable response yield
For an estimate or test, obtain the justified usable-sample requirement from a suitable precision or power plan. Do not default to 400. A bounded descriptive census may need coverage checks instead. [S6; S7; R4.]
With an identifiable invitation pool:
`expected usable completes = allowed unique invitations × usable-completion yield`
`Survey Viability = expected usable completes ÷ required usable completes`
This is an algebraic form of S6's invitation-resource ratio. Record the denominator of every rate. Apply eligibility, completion, and quality-loss factors only when they are separate conditional probabilities; never multiply the same loss twice.
Remove reserves and exclusions from the allowed pool. Remove overlapping exclusions once. Check priority groups separately. A respondent may belong to multiple groups while remaining one person overall. These are accounting requirements of the defined units, not a power model.
Use supported low/base/high inputs or explicitly labeled sensitivity assumptions. A ratio of 1 only matches the target in expectation. Higher values do not guarantee realized n, power, measurement validity, or coverage. For a vendor panel, request attainable eligible completes, quota feasibility, timing, and quality assumptions rather than inventing an invitation frame. [S6; conservative implementation.]
Power concerns detection of a specified effect under a specified design. Precision concerns uncertainty in an estimate. Detailed calculations, design effects, repeated measures, and subgroup requirements belong in the sampling handoff. Never count five answers from one person as five independent participants. [S7; S2, design; application.]
## R6 Handle tracking and decision risk
For recurring measures, check comparability of instrument, mode, recruitment, population, and interpretation. Plan how changes will be assessed. Avoid universal rules that instruments can never improve. For prediction, retain the additional validation requirements in R3. [S8.]
Relate methodological compromises to the consequence of being wrong. Make the resulting claim narrower when the evidence requires it. Time pressure does not change what the design establishes. Specialized methods require a defensible design and adequate analysis support. [S9; specialist-support requirement: package policy.]
## R7 Resolve source conflicts
Describe the conflict and the consequence for this case. The package does not adopt “why means interviews,” automatic rejection of small populations, universal psychometric cutoffs, or a response-yield guarantee. Sources S4, S6, S7, and S8 support different parts of the analysis; their shortcuts are not interchangeable.
When a consequential conclusion exceeds the inspected evidence, retrieve a suitable primary source or request qualified review. Mark the affected recommendation provisional. The citation and uncertainty requirements in SKILL.md continue to apply.
